CPRE 381 · COMPUTER ARCHITECTURE

MIPS
processors

A progression from foundational digital logic to a hazard-aware pipelined processor capable of running general-purpose instructions.

Building the foundation

The course began with building the CPU’s smaller logic components: adders, multiplexers, demultiplexers, and the supporting circuits that make a datapath work. I used those pieces to construct the initial datapath and develop a practical understanding of how information and control signals move through a processor.

Three processor designs

After the foundational work, I partnered with Charlie Zulk to build three increasingly capable MIPS processors. The first was a single-cycle processor with a datapath and control unit that completed one instruction per clock cycle. The second introduced pipelining, data-dependency detection, and the role of hazards in a faster processor. At that stage, we used NOP instructions to keep dependent instructions from conflicting in the pipeline.

Our final processor added the mechanisms needed to handle dependencies directly: stalling, flushing, and hazard control. That design could resolve conflicts without requiring NOPs in the assembly program, making the processor substantially more capable and closer to how real systems manage instruction flow.

Debugging and reflection

The most demanding part of the project was debugging. It required tracing each signal through the datapath and control logic, confirming where it originated, where it needed to go, and whether every connection was mapped correctly. The result made that effort worthwhile: seeing general-purpose instructions execute on a processor we had designed and built from the ground up was a memorable confirmation of how all the individual components came together.
